Troopers Hill, Bristol — eclipse sightline model

Partial solar eclipse, Wednesday 12 August 2026 · observer at 51.4562815°N, 2.5349075°W (summit, by the chimney) · terrain from EU-DEM 25 m · sun positions from NOAA solar-position algorithm

Verdict: yes — the WNW horizon is effectively flat from up there

Across the whole eclipse the sun sits between 20.6° and 3.9° above the true horizon, on bearings 268° to 289°. The terrain skyline on those bearings never rises above 0.35°. Worst-case clearance, at fourth contact, is +3.7° — about seven sun-widths. Every point of ground within 2.5 km is below your eye line in that direction. Terrain is not your problem. Cloud and the tree line are.

Key moments

What the model is and isn't

  • Terrain source: EU-DEM v1.1, 25 m posts, queried via OpenTopoData. 39 radial sightlines from 248° to 305°, 130 range samples each out to 60 km (25 m spacing near-field, coarsening with distance), plus two grids for the 3D mesh.
  • Who's on the skyline: from 254° to 293° it's the Failand ridge and Durdham Down, 6–12 km out at 87–163 m. From 294° westward — the last of the sunset arc — the nearby ground has dropped away far enough that the skyline becomes the Welsh hills 44–48 km across the Bristol Channel, 270–460 m high and still only 0.3° up. The sun sets over Monmouthshire.
  • Geometry: apparent altitude = atan((htarget − heye − d²/2R') / d), with R' = R/(1−k), k = 0.13 — the standard refraction coefficient. Eye height 1.6 m.
  • Sun positions computed independently with the NOAA algorithm and cross-checked against timeanddate.com: agreement within 0.1° on all three contact points.
  • The DEM puts this spot at 71 m, against a published summit of ~74 m — good agreement. (An earlier run used a coordinate 103 m to the south, on the shoulder at 58 m; every number here is from the true high point and is slightly better as a result.)
  • Bare earth only. EU-DEM is a terrain model. It contains no trees and no buildings.

The one thing this model can't tell you

At fourth contact the sun is 3.5° above the terrain skyline. At 200 m from where you're standing, 3.5° is about 12 m of height — one mature tree. The summit plateau by the chimney is open heath and the ground falls away steeply to the west and south-west toward the Avon, so this is very likely fine. But the last 20 minutes of the eclipse are the bit a single silver birch could ruin.

Worth two minutes on Street View or a walk-up beforehand: stand at the chimney, face WNW (285–290°), and check nothing organic is in the way. You're on the high point now — the ground falls away on every bearing in the sunset arc, so there's nowhere obviously better to move to. If a tree is in the way, the fix is lateral: step along the ridge until you can see past it.

Eyes

93.7% magnitude is not "nearly dark, therefore safe". At every moment of this eclipse there is un-eclipsed photosphere on view. Use ISO 12312-2 eclipse glasses, or a pinhole/binocular projection — never look through unfiltered binoculars, a camera viewfinder or a telescope. A low sun feels comfortable to look at, which is exactly what makes it dangerous.
